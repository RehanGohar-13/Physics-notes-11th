📖 Basic Concept of Scalars and Vectors

Scalars and vectors are basic concepts in physics. Many problems in physics require us to distinguish between scalar and vector quantities to apply the correct mathematical and conceptual approaches.

Understanding scalars and vectors helps us grasp how Physics applies to real-world situations — such as calculating the total distance travelled (scalar) or determining the magnitude and direction of force (vector).

Learning these concepts develops critical thinking and problem-solving skills. This chapter is primarily concerned with vector algebra and its application in:

  • Uniformly accelerated motion in a straight line
  • Motion of freely falling bodies in a uniform gravitational field
  • Projectile motion
  • Interaction between objects in one and two dimensions

2.1 Scalars

Scalars are physical quantities that are described solely by a magnitude (size or amount) without any mention of direction. Thus, scalars are directionless and can be fully characterized by a single number and its associated unit.

📌 Definition

A scalar has magnitude only. No direction is required for its complete specification.

Examples of Scalars

Quantity Meaning Example
Mass Amount of matter in an object 2 kg
Distance Total path length travelled (no direction) 50 m
Speed Rate of covering distance 40 km h⁻¹
Time Duration between two events 20 s
Energy Capacity to do work 25 J
Temperature Average KE of particles 20 °C

2.2 Vectors

Those physical quantities which require magnitude as well as direction for their complete specification are known as vectors.

📌 Definition

A vector has both magnitude and direction. Both are needed for complete specification.

Examples of Vectors

Quantity Meaning Example
Displacement Change in position (length + direction) 10 m towards west
Velocity Speed in a particular direction 50 km h⁻¹ towards west
Acceleration Rate of change of velocity (speed and/or direction) 10 m s⁻² upward
Force Push or pull (magnitude + direction) 20 N to the right

📏 Scalars

  • Magnitude only
  • No direction
  • Added by ordinary algebra
  • e.g. mass, time, energy

➡️ Vectors

  • Magnitude + direction
  • Direction matters
  • Added by vector rules
  • e.g. force, velocity, displacement

Graphical Representation of a Vector

A good way to represent a vector quantity is to use a vector diagram, in which vectors are often represented by arrows.

  • The length of the arrow indicates the magnitude
  • The head of the arrow shows the direction
  • Vectors are typically denoted by bold face letters (e.g. V, F) or an arrow above the symbol (e.g. Ā, F⃗)

Rectangular Components of a Vector

A component of a vector is its effective value in a given direction. A vector may be considered as the resultant of its component vectors along specified directions.

It is usually convenient to resolve a vector into its components along mutually perpendicular directions. Such components are called rectangular components.

Let there be a vector A represented by a line OP making an angle θ with the x-axis. Draw projection OM of vector A on the x-axis and projection ON of vector A on the y-axis (Fig. 2.1).

  • Projection OM along x-direction → Ax
  • Projection ON along y-direction → Ay

By applying head to tail rule:

Equation (2.1)
A = Ax + Ay
Ax and Ay are rectangular components of A (at right angles)

Considering the right-angled triangle OMP:

Equations (2.2) & (2.3)
Ax = A cos θ
Ay = A sin θ
Fig. 2.1: Vector A in a 2D Cartesian system with rectangular components Aₓ = A cos θ along x-axis (OM) and Aᵧ = A sin θ along y-axis (MP).

Determination of a Vector from its Rectangular Components

If the rectangular components of a vector are given, we can find the magnitude of the vector using the Pythagorean theorem.

In the right-angled ΔOMP:

Equation (2.4) — Magnitude
(OP)² = (OM)² + (MP)²
A² = Ax² + Ay²
A = √(Ax² + Ay²)

The direction θ is given by:

Equation (2.5) — Direction
tan θ = MP / OM = Ay / Ax
θ = tan⁻¹(Ay / Ax)

📝 Example 2.1

Worked Example
Find the angle between two forces of equal magnitude when the magnitude of their resultant is also equal to the magnitude of either of these forces.
Solution
Let θ be the angle between two forces F₁ and F₂, where F₁ is along the x-axis.

x-component of resultant:
Rₓ = F₁ cos 0° + F₂ cos θ
Rₓ = F₁ + F₂ cos θ

y-component of resultant:
Rᵧ = F₁ sin 0° + F₂ sin θ
Rᵧ = F₂ sin θ

Resultant: R² = Rₓ² + Rᵧ²

Given R = F₁ = F₂ = F, hence:
F² = (F + F cos θ)² + (F sin θ)²
F² = F² + F² cos²θ + 2F² cos θ + F² sin²θ
0 = 2F² cos θ + F² (cos²θ + sin²θ)
0 = 2F² cos θ + F² · 1
0 = F² (2 cos θ + 1)

As F ≠ 0:   2 cos θ + 1 = 0
cos θ = −0.5
θ = cos⁻¹(−0.5)

θ = 120°
💡
Exam tip

When two equal vectors have a resultant equal to either one, the angle between them is always 120°. This is a classic board result.

2.3 Product of Two Vectors

There are two types of vector multiplications. The products of these two types are known as:

  • Scalar product (dot product) — result is a scalar
  • Vector product (cross product) — result is a vector

Scalar or Dot Product

The scalar product of two vectors A and B is written as A · B and is defined as:

Equation (2.6) — Dot Product
A · B = AB cos θ
A and B are magnitudes; θ is the angle between them

For physical interpretation of the dot product of two vectors A and B, they are first brought to a common origin (Fig. 2.2-a). Then:

Interpretation
A · B = A × (projection of B on A)
or equivalently B × (projection of A on B)
Fig. 2.2 (a): Vector A along horizontal, vector B at angle θ, with projection B cos θ along A. Dot product A · B = A (B cos θ).
