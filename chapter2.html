📖 Basic Concept of Scalars and Vectors

Scalars and vectors are basic concepts in physics. Many problems in physics require us to distinguish between scalar and vector quantities to apply the correct mathematical and conceptual approaches.

Understanding scalars and vectors helps us grasp how Physics applies to real-world situations — such as calculating the total distance travelled (scalar) or determining the magnitude and direction of force (vector).

This chapter is primarily concerned with vector algebra and its application in uniform accelerated motion in a straight line, motion of freely falling bodies in a uniform gravitational field, projectile motion, and interaction between objects in one and two dimensions.

2.1 Scalars

Scalars are physical quantities that are described solely by a magnitude (size or amount) without any mention of direction. Thus, scalars are directionless and can be fully characterized by a single number and its associated unit.

📌 Definition

A scalar is a physical quantity specified completely by its magnitude and appropriate unit, requiring no direction.

Examples of Scalars

Quantity Meaning Example
Mass Amount of matter in an object 2 kg
Distance Total length of path travelled 50 m
Speed Rate at which an object covers distance 40 km h⁻¹
Time Duration between two events taking place 20 s
Energy Capacity to do work 25 J
Temperature Measure of average kinetic energy 20 °C

2.2 Vectors

Those physical quantities which require magnitude as well as direction for their complete specification are known as vectors.

📌 Definition

A vector is a physical quantity that requires both magnitude and direction for its complete specification.

Examples of Vectors

Quantity Meaning Example
Displacement Change in position 10 m towards west
Velocity Speed of an object in a particular direction 50 km h⁻¹ towards west
Acceleration Rate of change of velocity 10 m s⁻² upward
Force Push or pull acting on an object 20 N to the right

📏 Scalars

  • Magnitude only
  • No direction required
  • Added by ordinary arithmetic
  • e.g. mass, distance, speed, time

➡️ Vectors

  • Magnitude + Direction
  • Direction is mandatory
  • Added by vector algebra
  • e.g. force, velocity, acceleration

Graphical Representation of a Vector

A vector quantity is represented in a diagram by an arrow:

  • The length of the arrow indicates the magnitude.
  • The arrowhead indicates the direction.
  • Denoted by bold face letters (e.g. V, F) or a symbol with an arrow above it.

Rectangular Components of a Vector

A component of a vector is its effective value in a given direction. Resolving a vector along mutually perpendicular directions gives its rectangular components.

Equations (2.1), (2.2), (2.3)
A = Aₓ + Aᵧ   (Vector sum)
Aₓ = A cos θ   (x-component)
Aᵧ = A sin θ   (y-component)
Fig. 2.1: Vector A with rectangular components Aₓ and Aᵧ.

Determination of a Vector from its Rectangular Components

Using the Pythagorean Theorem in right-angled ΔOMP: (OP)² = (OM)² + (MP)²  ⟹  A² = Aₓ² + Aᵧ²

Equations (2.4) & (2.5)
A = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²)     (Magnitude)
θ = tan⁻¹(Aᵧ / Aₓ)     (Direction)

📝 Example 2.1

Worked Example
Find the angle between two forces of equal magnitude when the magnitude of their resultant is also equal to the magnitude of either of these forces.
Solution
Rₓ = F₁ cos 0° + F₂ cos θ = F₁ + F₂ cos θ
Rᵧ = F₁ sin 0° + F₂ sin θ = F₂ sin θ
R² = Rₓ² + Rᵧ²

Given R = F₁ = F₂ = F:
F² = (F + F cos θ)² + (F sin θ)²
F² = F² + F² cos²θ + 2F² cos θ + F² sin²θ
0 = F² (2 cos θ + 1)

Since F ≠ 0:   2 cos θ + 1 = 0  ⟹  cos θ = −0.5
θ = cos⁻¹(−0.5) = 120°

2.3 Product of Two Vectors

  • Scalar Product (Dot Product): Results in a scalar quantity.
  • Vector Product (Cross Product): Results in a vector quantity.

Scalar or Dot Product

Equation (2.6)
A · B = AB cos θ

Physical interpretation of dot product:

Projections Interpretation
A · B = A (component of B in direction of A) = AB cos θ
B · A = B (component of A in direction of B) = BA cos θ
Fig. 2.2 (a) & (b): Projection of B on A gives component B cos θ.

Physical Example — Work Done

Work as a Scalar Product
Work = (Effective component of force) × Distance
W = F · d = Fd cos θ
Fig. 2.3: Work done by a force F pulling a block through displacement d.

Characteristics of Scalar Product

1. Commutative Property

A · B = B · A

2. Perpendicular Vectors (θ = 90°)

A · B = AB cos 90° = 0

3. Parallel & Antiparallel

• Parallel (0°): A · B = AB
• Antiparallel (180°): A · B = −AB

4. Self Product

A · A = A²

5. Rectangular Components & Angle
A · B = AₓBₓ + AᵧBᵧ + AᴢBᴢ
cos θ = (AₓBₓ + AᵧBᵧ + AᴢBᴢ) / AB

Vector or Cross Product

The vector product of two vectors is a vector defined as:

Equation (2.9)
A × B = AB sin θ n̂
where n̂ is a unit vector perpendicular to the plane containing A and B.

Right Hand Rule: Place tails together. Rotate A into B. Curl fingers of right hand. The erect thumb points in the direction of the product vector.

Equation (2.10) — Anti-Commutative
A × B = − (B × A)

Characteristics of Cross Product

1. Non-Commutative

A × B ≠ B × A

2. Perpendicular (θ = 90°)

A × B = AB n̂ (Max magnitude)

3. Parallel/Antiparallel

A × B = 0 (Null Vector)

4. Self Cross Product

A × A = 0

5. Area of Parallelogram

|A × B| = Area of parallelogram
