Section 1.1
1.1 Physical Quantities and Their Units
Key Terms
Physical Quantity Base Quantities Derived Quantities Standard Measurement

The foundation of physics depends on physical quantities in terms of which the laws of Physics are expressed. Therefore, these quantities have to be measured accurately. These are mass, length, time, velocity, force, density, temperature, electric current, and numerous others.

Two Categories of Physical Quantities

🧱 Base Quantities

Not defined in terms of other physical quantities. Independent quantities that other physical quantities can be defined in terms of.
Examples: length, mass, time.

🔧 Derived Quantities

Depend on base quantities.
Examples: velocity, acceleration, force.

Steps in Measuring a Base Quantity

Step 1: The choice of a standard.
Step 2: The establishment of a procedure for comparing the quantity to be measured with the standard — so that a number and a unit are determined as the measure of that quantity.
Measurements must be reliable and accurate so that they can be used easily and effectively.

Section 1.2
1.2 International System of Units (SI)
Key Terms
SI System 1960 7 Base Units Global Standard

In 1960, an international committee agreed on a set of definitions and standards to describe the physical quantities. The system that was established is called the System International (SI).

SI units are used by the world's scientific community and by almost all nations. The System International (SI) consists of two kinds of units:

  • Base units
  • Derived units

Base Units

There are seven base units for physical quantities namely: length, mass, time, temperature, electric current, light or luminous intensity and amount of substance (with special reference to the number of particles). Prefixes such as milli, micro, kilo, etc. may be used with them to express smaller or larger quantities.

The names of base units for these physical quantities together with symbols are listed in Table 1.1.

📊 Table 1.1 — SI Base Units

Summary

The SI system (established 1960) is the global standard, built on 7 base units plus derived units. Prefixes let us scale them for very small or very large measurements.

📐 Derived Units
Key Terms
Derived Units Plane Angle (rad) Solid Angle (sr) Additional Units

Derived units are those units which depend on the base units. Some of the derived units are given in Table 1.2. The units of plane angle and solid angle have also been included in the list of derived units since 1995.

📊 Table 1.2 — Common Derived Units

Physical Quantity Unit Symbol In Base Units
Plane angle radian rad dimensionless
Solid angle steradian sr dimensionless
Force newton N kg m s⁻²
Work joule J N m = kg m² s⁻²
Power watt W J s⁻¹ = kg m² s⁻³
Electric charge coulomb C A s
Pressure pascal Pa N m⁻² = kg m⁻¹ s⁻²

Additional Permitted Units

In addition to base and derived units, the SI permits the use of certain additional units, including:

  • The traditional mathematical units for angles (degree, arcminute, arcsecond).
  • The traditional units for standard time (minute, hour, day, year).
  • The logarithmic units bel (and its multiples, such as the decibel).
  • Two metric units for ordinary life: litre for volume and tonne (metric ton) for large masses.
  • Two non-metric scientific units: atomic mass unit (μ) and the electron volt (eV).
  • The nautical mile and knot — used at sea and in meteorology.
  • The acre and hectare — common metric units of land area.
  • The bar — a unit of pressure (millibar in meteorology, kilobar in engineering).
  • The angstrom and the barn — units used in physics and astronomy.
Summary

Derived units come from combinations of base units. Since 1995, plane angle (rad) and solid angle (sr) are also included. SI also permits several traditional units (degree, litre, tonne, eV, etc.) for practical use.

🔢 Scientific Notation
Key Terms
Scientific Notation Standard Form Powers of Ten One Non-Zero Digit
Numbers are expressed in standard form called scientific notation, which employs powers of ten. The internationally accepted practice is that there should be only one non-zero digit left of the decimal.

Examples

134.7 → 1.347 × 10²
0.0023 → 2.3 × 10⁻³

🔤 Prefixes
Key Terms
Prefixes Steps of 1000 centi, deci, deca, hecto atto → exa

Most prefixes indicate order of magnitude in steps of 1000 and provide a convenient way to express large and small numbers, to eliminate non-significant digits. SI also includes four other prefixes to accommodate usage already established before the introduction of SI:

  • centi- (10⁻²)
  • deci- (10⁻¹)
  • deca- (10¹)
  • hecto- (10²)

✍️ Conventions for Using SI Units
Key Rules
Symbols not Abbreviations Lowercase Units Capital for Names No Plurals No Full Stops Spacing Rules

Use of SI units requires special care, more particularly in writing prefixes. Some points to note are:

1️⃣ Symbols, Not Abbreviations

Each SI unit is represented by a symbol, not an abbreviation. These symbols are the same in all languages.

Example: Use "A" not "amp"; "s" not "sec"; "SI" not "S.I."

2️⃣ Full Names — Lowercase

Full name of a unit does not begin with a capital letter.

Example: newton, metre, etc. — exception: Celsius.

3️⃣ Symbols — Lowercase

Symbols appear in lower case.

Example: "m" for metre, "s" for second. Exception: "L" for litre.

4️⃣ Named After Scientists → Capital

Symbols named after scientists have initial letters capital.

Example: "N" for newton, "Pa" for pascal, "W" for watt.

5️⃣ Upright (Roman) Style

Symbols and prefixes are printed in upright (roman) style regardless of the surrounding text style.

Example: a distance of 50 m.

6️⃣ No Plural Form

Symbols do not take plural form.

Example: 1 mm, 100 mm, 1 kg, 60 kg.

7️⃣ No Full Stops

No full stop or dot is placed after the symbol — except at the end of a sentence.

8️⃣ Prefix Attached to Unit

Prefix is written before and without a space to the base unit.

Example: "mL" not "m L"; "ms" not "m s".

9️⃣ Spacing Between Units & Values

Base units are written one space apart. Leave a space between the number (value) and the symbol.

Example: 1 kg, 10 m s⁻¹, etc.

🌟 Do You Know?
Mass can be thought of as a form of energy. In effect, mass is a highly concentrated form of energy. Einstein's famous equation, E = mc², means:
Energy = mass × (speed of light)²
According to this equation, 1 kg of mass is actually 9 × 10¹⁶ J of energy.
Summary

9 golden rules govern SI notation: use symbols (not abbreviations), lowercase names/symbols (except Celsius, litre, and units named after scientists), no plurals, no full stops, and always leave spaces between value and unit.
