📖 Introduction

Physics is the most fundamental branch of physical sciences. It provides the basic principles and laws which help to understand the mysteries of other branches of sciences such as astronomy, chemistry, geology, biology and health sciences.

The tools, techniques and products of Physics have transformed our dreams into realities. The comforts and pleasures added in our lives are fruitful results of science, technology and engineering in everyday life.

The Information technology has entirely changed the outlook of mankind. The fast means of communication have brought people of the entire world in so close contact that the whole world has become a global village.

Physics is an experimental science and the scientific method emphasizes the need of accurate measurement of various measurable physical quantities.

1.1 Physical Quantities and Their Units

The foundation of physics depends on physical quantities in terms of which the laws of Physics are expressed. These are mass, length, time, velocity, force, density, temperature, electric current, and numerous others.

📌 Key Concept

Base quantities are independent (not defined in terms of others). Derived quantities depend on base quantities.

Category Definition Examples
Base Independent; other quantities defined from these Length, Mass, Time
Derived Depend on base quantities Velocity, Acceleration, Force

The measurement of a base quantity involves two steps: first, the choice of a standard, and second, the establishment of a procedure for comparing the quantity to be measured with the standard.

📝 Conventions for Using SI Units

All 16 rules you need for your board exams:

  1. Symbol, not abbreviation. Same in all languages.✅ A not "amp" · s not "sec" · SI not "S.I."
  2. Full name: no capital letter.✅ newton, metre · Exception: Celsius
  3. Symbols: lower case.✅ m, s · Exception: L for litre
  4. Named after scientist → capital initial.✅ N, Pa, W
  5. Upright (roman) style always.✅ 50 m (not italic)
  6. No plural form.✅ 100 mm, 60 kg · ❌ 100 mms
  7. No full stop after symbol (except end of sentence).
  8. Prefix: before unit, no space.✅ mL, ms · ❌ m L
  9. Space between value and symbol.✅ 1 kg, 10 m s⁻¹ · ❌ 1kg
  10. No compound prefixes.✅ 1 pF · ❌ 1 μμF
  11. Power applies to whole multiple.✅ 1 km² = 1 × (10³ m)² = 10⁶ m²
  12. Use negative index, not solidus.✅ m s⁻¹ · ❌ m/s
  13. Scientific notation: one non-zero digit left of decimal.✅ 1.437 × 10² · ❌ 143.7
  14. Don't mix symbols and names.✅ m s⁻¹ or "metre per second" · ❌ m/second
  15. Record in convenient units, convert to base for final answer.Screw gauge → mm during work, → m in final result
  16. CGS units not allowed in SI.❌ dyne, erg, gauss, poise, torr
⚠️
Board Exam Alert!

SI conventions are frequently tested in Inter/FSc. "Identify the incorrect usage" questions appear regularly!

1.3 Uncertainty in Measurement

You can count the pages of a book exactly, but measuring its length needs an instrument. Every instrument is calibrated to a certain smallest division, which puts a limit on its accuracy.

📌 Absolute Uncertainty

The maximum uncertainty of a measurement — usually equal to one smallest division (the least count) of the instrument.

Uncertainty in Digital Instruments

With a digital scale, uncertainty is reflected in fluctuations of the last digit. If the last digit fluctuates by 1–2, write it down. If more than 2, the reading may be influenced by external factors (e.g., air currents).

1.4 Use of Significant Figures

The number of digits about which we feel reasonably sure are called significant figures. They reflect the actual instrument used.

📌 Definition

In any measurement, the accurately known digits and the first estimated/doubtful digit are called significant figures.

Example — weighing the same object with different balances:

Instrument Reading Sig Figs Precision
Electronic balance 3.145 ± 0.001 g 4 Higher ✅
Lever balance 3.1 ± 0.1 g 2 Lower

(i) Counting Significant Digits

  • All non-zero digits (1–9) are always significant
  • Zero between two sig figs is significant (e.g., 3005 → 4 sig figs)
  • Leading zeros are NOT significant (e.g., 0.00467 → 3 sig figs)
  • Trailing zeros after decimal ARE significant (e.g., 3.570 → 4 sig figs)
  • Trailing zeros in integers depend on instrument precision (e.g., 8000 kg could be 1–4 sig figs — use scientific notation!)
  • In scientific notation, only the coefficient digits count (e.g., 8.70 × 10⁴ → 3 sig figs)

(ii) Multiplying or Dividing

Keep sig figs equal to the least accurate factor (fewest sig figs).

Example
(5.348×10⁻² × 3.64×10⁴) / 1.336 = 1.46 × 10³
3.64 has 3 sig figs (fewest) → answer rounded to 3 sig figs

Rounding Rules

  • Dropped digit < 5 → last retained digit unchanged
  • Dropped digit > 5 → last retained digit increases by 1
  • Dropped digit = 5 → round to even (increase if odd, keep if even)
Original Rounded (3 sig figs) Rule Applied
43.75 43.8 5 → round up (7 is odd)
56.8546 56.9 Dropped 5 → round up (8 is even... wait, 546 > 5 so round up)
73.650 73.6 5 → keep (6 is even)
64.350 64.4 5 → round up (3 is odd)

⚠️ Limitations of Significant Figures

Significant figures only deal with one source of uncertainty — reading the scale. Real experiments have many contributions: personal errors, hidden systematic errors, etc. Total uncertainty may be more than what sig figs suggest!

1.5 Precision and Accuracy

These two terms are often confused — but they're completely different!

🎯 Precision

Determined by the instrument. Smaller least count = more precise. Measured by absolute uncertainty.

🎯 Accuracy

Closeness to the true value. Measured by fractional / percentage uncertainty.

Periodic uncertainty → divide least count of timing device by number of oscillations

💥 Did You Know?

🤯
Mass IS Energy!

Mass can be thought of as a highly concentrated form of energy. Einstein's famous equation shows the equivalence:

Einstein's Mass-Energy Equivalence
E = mc²
Energy = mass × (speed of light)²
⚡
Mind-Blowing Fact

Just 1 kg of mass = 9 × 10¹⁶ J of energy — enough to power a nuclear plant for years!

  • Prefixes indicate powers of 10, mostly in steps of 1000
  • 16 SI conventions for writing units correctly
  • Absolute uncertainty = smallest division of instrument (least count)
  • Fractional uncertainty = Δx / x, Percentage = (Δx / x) × 100%
  • Digital instruments show uncertainty as fluctuation in last digit
  • Add/Subtract → match decimal places
